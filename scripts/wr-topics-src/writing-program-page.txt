WR 111 and WR 112 Courses (for Multilingual Students)
CAS WR 111
Academic Writing for ELL Students
4 credits. Fall and Spring

BU Hub Learn More
The Individual in Community
Undergraduate Prerequisites: placement results. - Study of academic conventions and effective strategies of academic reading and writing, along with needs-based review of grammar and mechanics. Emphasis on comprehension, summary, and analysis. Focus on fluency and accuracy in writing and speaking. Frequent papers and in-class writing. Effective Fall 2018, this course fulfills a single unit in the following BU Hub area: The Individual in Community.

CAS WR 112
Critical Literacies for ELL
4 credits. Fall and Spring

BU Hub Learn More
Global Citizenship and Intercultural Literacy
Undergraduate Prerequisites: CASWR 111, placement results, or transfer credit for WR 01TR. - Critical reading and analytical writing in response to various theme-based texts. Review of grammar and mechanics in context. Practice in the patterns of academic argumentation through multiple assignments of increasing complexity. Refinement of speaking skills through discussions and oral presentations. Effective Fall 2018, this course fulfills a single unit in the following BU Hub area: Global Citizenship and Intercultural Literacy.

Topic Title	Topic Description	Instructor	Class
Agency Across the Ages	What shapes human action? In this seminar, we explore agency: the capacity to act, choose, resist, and take responsibility. We ask how people act under pressures they do not fully control, from fate and law to desire, memory, and technology. Reading ancient, modern, literary, and philosophical texts, we consider what makes an action truly one’s own, whether responsibility can exceed control, and how tools and environments shape who we are. The course invites us to connect ancient questions about freedom to contemporary questions about agency and decision-making in everyday life.	Oser	WR 120
Arab Culture Through Film	This course introduces Arab culture with a focus on key historical and social issues through the lens of Arabic films, both as works of art and as cultural texts. We explore the diversity of Arab culture through this distinct art form that reflects, interprets, critiques, reshapes, and impacts culture. We analyze and critically examine films across a variety of genres, addressing such themes as national identity, colonialism, conflicts, trauma, religion, ethnic and linguistic diversity, and Western portrayal of Arabs/Arab culture in film. We identify and question racial bias to reflect upon the varied experiences of people around the Arab World.	Slimane	WR 120
Art and Social Change	We explore the ways in which art can transform societal constrictions into new opportunities. Through this exploration, we examine various modes of artistic expression and reflect upon the changes they have enacted in society. We explore modes of writing and expression around this topic.	Davidoff	WR 120
Becoming White/Non-White	This course examines how people become “white” and “non-white” through modern racial formations. We explore the historical formation of these categories in the nineteenth century and how they are reproduced as American racial categories disseminated globally through scholarship, media, medicine, and education. Treating race as a historical and relational process rather than a fixed identity, the course examines how racial categories are reproduced and made to appear natural through institutions, language, medical classifications, slurs, addiction discourse, and race-reductionist scholarship. Particular attention is given to how progressive frameworks can reproduce the very color line they seek to critique.	Pasto	WR 120
Bioethics and Identity	This course explores the intersections of bioethics, race and gender. Such essential topics raise issues of discrimination, justice, oppression, critical race bioethics, and diverse feminist principles within the values, histories and practices of STEM fields. Who is included or excluded in accessing proper healthcare, along with the advancement of medical and biotechnology? Who or what is highlighted or erased? What are the lived experiences of women and minorities in STEM fields, both historically and today? We draw from social and biological sciences, public health, biotech, medicine and public policy to better examine and understand the racial and gender-specific concerns involved.	Lynch	WR 120
Boston Jazz Now	We explore Jazz music, with a focus on Boston as a leading center for Jazz in the US. Topics include: the evolution and history of Jazz; its spread to different regions of the country; its major subgenres and styles; great Jazz musicians, bandleaders, and critics; connections between Jazz and other musical genres; and the development of Jazz in Boston, with special attention to Boston’s musicians, musical styles, schools, and clubs, both past and present. We attend live concerts in person at venues such as Berklee College of Music, the New England Conservatory, and BU’s College of Fine Arts.	Oller	WR 120
Boston Sports Now	Why are so many Bostonians so utterly obsessed with sports? How does the viewing public identify with teams, players, and places? What are the lasting impacts of sports on Boston’s cityscape? Considering Boston sports culture and social history, we reflect on our own engagement with sports and Boston sports venues, teams, media, monuments, and fans. Identifying and experiencing real and imagined neighborhoods and communities, we visit sites of contemporary and historical significance, listen to sports talk radio, and attend BU sports games.	White	WR 120
Chronically Online	This course explores the digital world and its impact on the social world. How does what happens online extend into the offline world? How ought we to treat one another online? Can real harms occur through digital means, and how ought we to address such harms? Throughout the course we look at ongoing and emerging issues, such as online echo chambers, deep fakes, radicalized communities, digital freedom, and so on. Drawing primarily on texts in social philosophy and feminist theory, we confront both practical and theoretical questions surrounding the collision of the online and offline world.	Looijaard	WR 120
Communicating Science	Information changes and evolves as it is communicated to different audiences through different media. This course focuses on how scientific information is presented and interpreted by a general audience that does not have technical knowledge. By examining press releases, news articles, government guidelines, and social media, we explore how scientific information is disseminated to the public and uncover what it takes to be scientifically literate in a twenty-first-century world. Course texts include works by authors such as Michael Pollen and Mariam Nestle and a variety of traditional and multimodal sources, such as blogs, podcasts, and even pop songs.	Calandra	WR 120
Ecology of Global Englishes	We use language every day, but how often do we stop to think about what exactly language is? Yes, we produce sounds and/or signs and movements which come together to create sequences which carry meaning. Similarly, we write—putting together letters, words, and sentences. But how is meaning attached to sounds, signs, and letters? How does a language come to be in the first place? And what ideologies are placed onto language and language users? In this class, we will explore these questions by examining the history of and relationship between power and language with a specific focus on Englishes.	Sun Han Chang	WR 120
Fiber Arts and Culture	We consider (and may experiment with) weaving, embroidery, knitting, crochet, and more, analyzing them through North American cultural history and questions of race, class, and gender. Are these “granny hobbies,” sites of creativity and innovation, tactile representations of advanced mathematics, or something more? We may discuss topics as varied as the Lowell mill girls, the AIDS memorial quilt, tradwife influencers, and the Etsy economy. Throughout the semester, we explore how yarn, fabric, and thread can tell powerful human stories while also intersecting with fine art, activism, and sustainability.	Michaud	WR 120
Flannery O’Connor’s Grotesque	In this class, we look at the works of Flannery O’Connor, through her short stories, novel, essays, prayers, and private writings. Our examination will reveal, in her words, a “tainted” series of influences and events hidden within the material, such as the impact of the Civil Rights Movement, the dying of the Post Reconstruction South, the rise of Evangelical Christianity, and the influence of Black Americans on Southern food and music. O’Connor uses her deep understanding of Catholic theology and existential philosophy to view her home and the changing times through her “tainted” lens.	Morazzini	WR 120
Food and Cultural Identity	The course will examine the connection between cultural identity with food and how it is part of what defines a community and an individual.  The materials presented will cover the influence of history, colonization,immigration and climate change as forces that shape what we eat and ultimately how we define ourselves.	Drepanos	WR 120
Hollywood Contradicts Itself	In 1915, Hollywood produced the massively popular white supremacist film The Birth of a Nation. A century later, Hollywood produced the massively popular Black Panther (2018). What factors motivated this supposedly radical change? How and why are these films different, artistically or politically? What makes them similar? Moreover, what do we even mean when we say “Hollywood,” given that it has gone through so many artistic, technological, political, and institutional changes that have resulted in films that look, sound, and mean different. Our task in this course is to explore several possible understandings of what Hollywood is by accounting for the contradictions that animate it. By looking at movies as diverse as A Star is Born (2018 ), Jaws (1975), and Black Panther, we can see Hollywood as a space of competing tensions, including but not limited to: Art versus entertainment, inclusion v. exclusion, innovation v. stasis, conservative anxieties v. liberal affirmations, and the national v. the international.	Vanaria	WR 120
Memoir As Art Form	This course offers an engaging exploration into the art of memoir writing, guiding us through the process of crafting our own life stories. Through the study of acclaimed memoirs, we will gain insights into various narrative techniques and thematic elements. We will also participate in writing short memoirs, allowing us to practice and refine our storytelling skills. The course also includes two analytical papers, where we will critically examine the memoirs studied, and a presentation to develop your public speaking and interpretive skills. Additionally, an alternative assignment will challenge us to compose our own memoirs, providing a comprehensive understanding of the memoir genre. By the end of the course, we will have a deeper appreciation for the memoir as a literary form and we will have developed our own unique voice and style in writing personal narratives.	Westhues	WR 120
Metaphor & Advertising	This course explores the powerful role of visual metaphor in shaping consumer perception and behavior, examining how advertisers deploy imagery, language, and design to craft compelling messages that shape consumer desire and drive purchasing decisions. Drawing on Conceptual Metaphor Theory and Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs, we analyze contemporary advertisements to understand how marketers align visual and verbal metaphors with fundamental human desires for security, connection, achievement, and fulfillment. Through close reading, research, and argumentative writing, we develop the critical tools to decode visual rhetoric in mass media, identify the psychological strategies behind advertising, and evaluate the ethics of persuasion	O’Mara	WR 120
Migration Culture	How does the phenomenon of human migration affect culture, and what new cultures are created as people are displaced and migrate? In this course, we will study how migration–and, in particular, forced displacement—is represented in culture, both globally and locally: in what we read and watch, in the ways we communicate, in how we think about space and place, and in the art and music we create and consume. You’ll delve into the culture of migration with your own research and explorations outside the classroom, working in academic and multimedia genres, and connecting with migration culture here in Boston through museum exhibits, public art, and local events.	Kordonowy	WR 120
Optimizing Personal Wellness	What happens to the mind and body when exposed to different stressors? What techniques have humans developed over centuries to mitigate the negative effects of stress and maintain a healthy balance? In this course, we examine how a range of mindfulness practices, including yoga and meditation, can combine with healthy eating practices to optimize personal wellness.	Fincke	WR 120
Science Communication	This course examines the kinds of writing involved in scientific discoveries from grant proposals, through research notes, to publication, and finally public dissemination. We look at a few high-profile studies such as the research on Chronic Traumatic Encephalopathy in NFL players and the public discourse on these findings. Students write about the differing presentations of information for different audiences and purposes. We also explore how error and misunderstanding regarding scientific discovery can develop.	Kinraide	WR 120
Time Travel in Fiction	Would you—and should you—travel through time? Physics may limit us, but fiction sets us free. In this course, we examine time travel as a narrative tool, analyzing stories, film, and TV shows to uncover the paradoxes, possibilities, and ethical stakes embedded in the genre. From time loops to alternate histories, we interrogate what travel to the past and future reveals about the present. The course culminates in a final project: a trailer for an original time-travel film, set in Boston during a historical event of the students’ choice.	Mazur-Jeffries	WR 120
Translation and Adaptation	Which was better – the film or the book? What do hygge and treppenwitz mean anyway? In this course we explore what happens when we convert an original source into another language or genre, through translation or adaptation. What’s lost? What’s gained? Does a successful transformation require literal fidelity or a more nuanced touch? Students investigate these questions across various genres and linguistic experiences. After establishing some theoretical groundwork, we critique several adaptations and translations of texts and students have the opportunity to create their own as well.	Hanselman	WR 120
Twenty-First Century Terrors	In the 60s and 70s, Vietnam and civil unrest gave birth to a golden age of horror movies. In our new century, we have been forced to confront tragedy on an even greater level. This course examines the horror genre in both film and television as a powerful meditation on the fears of a new and uncertain century, including terrorism, chemical and biological warfare, pandemic, authoritarianism, and nuclear Armageddon. Attention is given to works made in the US, Europe, and Southeast Asia to consider the genre in a global, cross-cultural context.	Vahamikos	WR 120
Who Owns the Past Now?	Museums, historical sites, and universities collect, preserve, and present objects and places to tell the story of our past and present. What these institutions choose to collect and how they interpret them constructs and transmits human history, culture, and memory. In this course, we explore how items are collected, the ethics of curation, and the power within being able to create and tell the stories of humanity’s past and present. Site visits, multimedia analysis, and personal reflection provide groundwork for us to ask: which stories are being told, and which stories may be altered, misrepresented, or ignored?	Backs	WR 120
Writing Environmental Justice	What makes people vulnerable to environmental harm, including the harms of global heating? How can it be mitigated? Literature raises these critical questions and offers thoughtful answers. It also provides much-needed vision and hope. In this course, we closeread written and film texts to ground our thinking about environmental abuses and the people they impact most. We examine how contemporary texts illuminate environmental racism and consumption. We also ask: How can each of us participate as change agents in enacting environmental justice? The authors guiding our investigation may include Octavia Butler, Robin Wall Kimmerer, Robert Bullard, and Janice Mirikitani.	Tall	WR 120

1968-1969: Hope and Horror	This course examines the social and political upheavals that rocked American society in 1968-1969: protests against the Vietnam War, struggles for gender equality and sexual freedom, and powerful movements for racial justice, framed by the assassinations of Martin Luther King, Jr. in March 1968 and Fred Hampton in December 1969. The first half of the course immerses you in the role of a delegate, protestor, or journalist at the Chicago Democratic National Convention in August 1968. In the second half of our course, we shift our gaze westward and to 1969, tracing the countercultural revolution in California.	McDonough	WR 151

Addiction in Horror Media	Though the lived experience of addiction can be horrifying in its own right, artists have often used gothic tropes like vampires, zombies, and demonic possessions to explore the compulsions and obsessions of addicted people. In this course, we will examine these metaphorical addicts alongside more literal treatments of chemical and behavioral addiction in horror media. We will ask: how have horror tropes and storylines shaped public perceptions of addiction? How do categories of gender, race, and sexuality inform representations of uncontrollable consumption? And in what ways do horror texts engender compassion for addicted people and not just fear of them?	Bjornson	WR 152

We will examine these concerns and more by studying the conversations film scholars have had about Hollywood, and by conducting research of our own to understand how Hollywood is animated by competing tensions, including but not limited to: Art versus entertainment, inclusion v. exclusion, innovation v. stasis, conservative anxieties v. liberal affirmations, and the national v. the international. Known for their form and originality, the poems of these Massachusetts poets are frequently read but often misread. Many of Dickinson and Plath’s poems were altered and published without the poets’ knowledge after their deaths. Their lives are compelling enough to have spurred hundreds of books and representations in art, film, and television. But how accurate are our imaginings of these poets? How have their legacies endured and will they last through the ages? Through our research and writing, we will come to our own understanding of who these poets were and what their work means.
